Four-Part Harmony

A family of buttons: four teammates become a band.

Design question: how can one button per teammate show who each person is, and how the team works together? Tapping wakes a teammate as a musician. A hobby sets their instrument; a Big Five personality survey sets their tempo, timing and energy. Wake two or more and their rhythms drift or sync. Drag them closer to strengthen the connection.

Skills I practised Low-fidelity sketching · divergent ideation · criteria-based comparison · heuristic walkthroughs · design writing

Team: Jawad Naheen Shamim, Hardik Mehta, Shashwat Tank, and Happy Prajapati. The system and process are the team’s shared work; the contributions above are mine.

  1. Step 01 · Diverge

    Explore many identities

    We started with one quick concept per teammate (anime, gym, travel, events), then a shared room. Each idea was recognizable, but each behaved like a separate profile. None showed the team.

    My interface sketch tested a stage where four sleeping teammates wait for a tap to wake up.

  2. Step 02 · Converge

    Choose one shared metaphor

    We compared four directions against criteria from the brief: identity, team dynamics, interaction potential, novelty, clarity, and feasibility. Only the band was strong on both identity and team dynamics. Sound shows the individual; sync shows the relationship.

    Each character got a small prop that hints at their personality. Mine wears a headset, a nod to my event work.

  3. Step 03 · Prototype

    Make relationships tangible

    Personality became behaviour: activity sets tempo, organization sets timing precision, extraversion sets size and bounce, agreeableness sets how readily a rhythm follows others.

    Watching wasn’t enough, so we added dragging: proximity sets how strongly two teammates influence each other, and people can test whether a pair will sync.

  4. Step 04 · Evaluate & refine

    Make the feedback readable

    Walkthroughs and a heuristic review showed where first-time users got lost. We acted on what we found:

    • Status unclear → one consistent set of states: Waiting, Drifting, In sync.
    • “% alike” meant nothing → “personality similarity”, with the closest and most different traits.
    • Text-heavy panel → compact cards, with detail moved into a collapsible section.

What I carried forward: behaviour can carry meaning, but people need a short label to confirm what they are seeing.

AirSculpt

Remote 3D sculpting, taught through movement.

Design question: how can an expert teach the movement behind a sculpting technique from a distance? AirSculpt turns a fingertip and a pinch into a live 3D demonstration. The learner sees a translucent ghost of the expert’s stroke, practises on their own clay, and gets feedback on where and how to correct it.

Skills I practised Concept matrices · task analysis · storyboarding · heuristic inspection · design rationale writing

Team: Jawad Naheen Shamim, Hardik Mehta, Shashwat Tank, and Happy Prajapati. The system and process are the team’s shared work; the contributions above are mine.

  1. Step 01 · Diverge & compare

    Pick an input anyone can use

    We sketched three embodied inputs: a phone as a wand, a tracked stylus, and webcam hand tracking. Then we compared them for expressiveness, teaching value, and equipment needs. Webcam pinch won: it needs only a laptop.

    The index finger places the brush, and a deliberate pinch starts a stroke, so pointing never edits by accident. A labelled mouse mode keeps the lesson going when tracking fails.

  2. Step 02 · Prototype

    Teach through a ghost

    The expert’s stroke streams to the learner as a translucent ghost, while the learner’s clay stays separate. Watching never completes the attempt for them.

    Private sessions set up expert and novice roles, and a replay lets the learner review the movement as often as they need.

  3. Step 03 · Test & refine

    Show the next correction

    The first build exposed a misleading metric: a global score rated untouched clay at about 97%. Scoring only the region the expert edited made progress reflect the skill being taught.

    A single tint didn’t say what to do, so feedback became directional: blue = Pull, orange-red = Push. The expert can also point at the learner’s clay without changing it.

  4. Step 04 · Extend

    Bring the whole class in

    Students join by QR code and sculpt their own clay on their phones. A live class wall lets the expert watch everyone’s progress at once.

    The teaching cycle stays the same, just shared: demonstrate, watch, practise, review.

What I carried forward: embodied input alone isn’t enough. Feedback has to tell the learner what to change, where, and why.

Six-iteration log & evaluation scope
  1. It. 1
    Teaching loop

    Expert/novice roles, separate clay, webcam calibration, mouse fallback, streamed ghost.

  2. It. 2
    Directional feedback

    Region-aware score, Pull/Push heatmap, live Point & guide.

  3. It. 3
    Pressure & sound

    Hand distance sets brush strength; stroke beads show it; optional audio tracks progress.

  4. It. 4
    Beyond one sphere

    Four starting objects, easier rotation, Reset view, on-screen instructions.

  5. It. 5
    Visual hierarchy

    Floating panels over a full-bleed stage so the clay stays dominant.

  6. It. 6
    Whole class

    QR join, phone sculpting, live class wall.

Evaluation scope: prototype walkthroughs and heuristic inspection. Shape match is a geometric metric; we don’t claim measured learning gains. Some process sketches were drawn afterwards to explain decisions. Session state is temporary.

03 · How my design grew

From showing a state to guiding the next move
