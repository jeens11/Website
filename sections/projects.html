AirSculpt

Remote 3D sculpting,
taught through movement.

How can an expert teach the movement behind a sculpting technique from a distance? AirSculpt turns fingertip movement and a pinch into a live 3D demonstration. The learner sees a translucent ghost, practises on their own clay, and receives feedback on where and how to correct it.

My contributionDesign and documentation. I contributed to AirSculpt’s design and helped document the team’s process and design decisions.

Team project: Jawad Naheen Shamim, Hardik Mehta, Shashwat Tank, and Happy Prajapati. The process and system described here are the team’s collective work.

01 / EXPLORE

Compare input ideas

Consider phone motion, a tracked stylus, and webcam gestures against expressiveness, teaching value, and equipment needs.

02 / PROTOTYPE

Teach through a ghost

Stream the expert’s stroke while keeping the learner’s clay separate, so observing a demonstration never completes their attempt.

03 / REFINE

Show the next correction

Replace an ambiguous score with a task-region comparison, Pull/Push colours, and live expert pointing.

04 / EXTEND

Bring the class in

Add QR joining, individual phone practice, and a class wall so a projected demonstration can become a shared activity.

Design takeaway: A demonstration becomes more useful when feedback tells the learner what to change, where, and why.

Explore the design process and iterations

From expressive input to useful guidance

The team converged on webcam input because it works with a standard laptop. The index finger locates the brush; a deliberate thumb–index pinch starts a stroke. Tool selection stays explicit, and a mouse fallback preserves the lesson when tracking is unavailable.

These are team process figures from the submitted report. Some figures explain decisions retrospectively; they are not records of a participant study.

Three decisions that changed the experience

Iterations 1–2 / Teaching loop
Keep demonstration and practice independent

Private sessions establish expert and novice roles. A ghost and replay communicate the target; signed feedback and a pointer support the learner’s own attempt.

Iteration 3 / Embodied output
Make pressure visible

Calibrated hand distance modulates brush strength. Stroke beads vary with strength, and optional audio gives another channel for progress feedback.

Iterations 4–5 / Control & hierarchy
Make the workspace easier to understand

Four starting objects, clear orbit instructions, Reset view, and a refined panel hierarchy keep attention on the clay while preserving control.

Iteration 6 / Shared participation
Scale the same lesson

Class mode keeps the same demonstrate–watch–practise–review cycle. Each student works on an independent object using phone touch.

Evaluation and scope: The report documents prototype walkthroughs and heuristic inspection. Shape match is a geometric demonstration metric; the project does not claim measured learning gains. AirSculpt teaches Blender-like sculpting in its own web application, and session state is temporary.
